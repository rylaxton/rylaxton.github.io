53 g Spring-Lever Mechanism (ME 104)

A 53 g lever-and-spring mechanism built to resist a closing hatch with a 4,000 N/m linear force response over a 50 mm stroke. It reached 186 N of a required 200 N and returned 33 N RMS force error, about three quarters of which traces to 13 mm of slack at the start of the stroke rather than to any shortfall in force.

Project overview

The second ME 104 design project asks for a spring-like widget that resists a hatch as it closes, producing a specified force-displacement relationship at the lowest possible mass. The scenario is a hatch on a Mars habitat, so mass is expensive and the widget sees only a few load cycles, which makes fatigue explicitly not a design driver. Each team is issued one catalog spring of its choosing and builds everything else.

The device has to fit between two fixed interfaces and touch them only where permitted: a pin coupling above, and a platen below where contact is allowed on an outer annulus and nowhere else, including under deflection at full load. Testing is on an MTS load frame, which weighs the device, checks geometric compliance, then drives the upper interface down through the stroke and back while recording reaction force. Both directions count toward the error figure.

The other ME 104 project on this site, the motor-driven impulse machine, used the same load frame and the same 50 mm stroke to a different end: maximizing delivered impulse rather than tracking a prescribed force curve at minimum mass.

Objective and requirements

Produce a vertical reaction force rising linearly from 0 N at first contact to 200 N at full displacement, hold it on both halves of the stroke, and do it at minimum mass.

  • Force: 4,000 N/m, or 0 to 200 N across the stroke, matched going down and coming back up
  • Stroke: upper interface travels from 0.20 m to 0.15 m separation and back, a 50 mm displacement
  • Failure: a peak force below 100 N at maximum displacement, or 50% force error, for any reason
  • Envelope and contact: the part may touch any part of the pin coupling cylinder but must not extend above it, and may touch the platen only on the outer annulus of its top face, including under deflection at full load
  • Mass: minimized, with the provided spring excluded from the scored figure
  • Springs: one spring from three catalog options, and no other catalog component may provide substantial compliance or energy storage

My role

Two-person team, with testing shared. I owned everything downstream of the device's geometry: resolving the reactions and member loads, inverse sizing each member against its governing failure mode, the CAD assembly, the FEA and buckling studies, and fabrication and assembly.

The 2D analysis and the geometry optimization that set the device's proportions were my partner's work rather than mine, and the 53 g mass and 186 N peak are a team result.

Engineering process

  1. Requirements. Taken from the assignment. No target mass or target error was published in advance, so we set our own working targets from what the analysis suggested was reachable.
  2. Spring selection. We chose the extension spring, McMaster-Carr 5667N356, from the three offered. [Add why extension rather than the compression or die spring, and what the back-of-the-envelope comparison showed about each.]
  3. Geometry optimization. My partner ran a parametric sweep over the geometry variables, scoring each candidate by squared deviation from the 4,000 N/m target line. The leading candidates predicted a match on the order of 1 N RMS, with 199.3 N of reaction at full stroke and 260.3 N in the spring.
  4. Load resolution. From the selected geometry I resolved the support reactions and then the member loads, taking the reaction split from the overall free body diagram and the axial and bending loads from member-level diagrams.
  5. Inverse sizing. Rather than picking sections and checking them, I solved each failure criterion for the section it required: axial, bending, the two combined, and Euler buckling. Combined loading governed, at roughly 35 MPa against a 40 MPa allowable.
  6. CAD and FEA. I modelled the assembly and ran static stress and linear buckling studies, checking the FEA stress distribution against the members the inverse analysis had flagged as critical.

Analysis methods and assumptions

  • Member free body diagrams were resolved under a small-angle assumption, reasonable at the working geometry but degrading as the lever rotates through the stroke
  • Failure modes considered: axial yield, bending yield, the two combined, and Euler buckling of the compression members
  • Fatigue was not analyzed. The assignment states the widget sees only a few load cycles, so this is a permitted simplification, but nothing here speaks to repeated use

Test procedure

  1. Device weighed, with the provided spring excluded from the recorded mass
  2. Geometric compliance checked against the pin coupling envelope and the permitted platen contact annulus
  3. Device installed between the pin coupling and the platen
  4. Upper interface driven slowly from 0.20 m to 0.15 m separation and back, with reaction force recorded continuously against displacement
  5. Pass criteria: peak force at or above 100 N, and force error within 50%, counting both halves of the stroke

Results

  • Passed the geometric compliance check, so it was scored on performance rather than disqualified on contact or envelope
  • 53 g excluding the provided spring
  • 186 N peak reaction at full displacement against a 200 N requirement and a 100 N failure threshold: 93% of target, 86% above the floor
  • 33 N RMS force error across both halves of the stroke
  • Peak force landed 6.6% below the 199.3 N predicted, good agreement for a hand-built mechanism, but the force error came in roughly thirty times the model's prediction

Where the force error came from

The device produced almost no reaction force over the first part of the stroke. Reading the recorded curve, force stays below 5 N for the first 12.9 mm going down and the first 14.2 mm coming back up, so roughly a quarter of the 50 mm stroke did no work. Because error is scored against a line through the origin, that is expensive: the target curve already calls for about 52 N at 13 mm of displacement, and the deficit accumulated there propagates through the rest of the stroke.

The size of that effect is worth being precise about. Holding the measured 186 N peak fixed and asking what the error would have been had the device engaged at zero displacement and risen linearly to that same peak gives roughly 8 N RMS instead of 33 N. So the 14 N shortfall in peak force accounts for only a small part of the error. Most of it is the delayed engagement.

The most likely cause is slack in the extension spring, which has to be taken up before the lever begins to load the frame at all. Nothing in the geometry model accounted for it, because the model began at first contact by construction.

The curve also encloses a hysteresis loop of about 0.84 J against roughly 3.97 J of loading work, near 21% lost per cycle, consistent with friction at the pivots and hysteresis in the spring, though we did not separate the two. Since both halves of the stroke counted toward force error, that loss fed straight into the measured result.

The onset displacements, the counterfactual error, and the loop area above were recovered by tracing the recorded force-displacement plot rather than from the raw data file. The trace reproduces the reported 186 N peak and 33 N RMS error to within about one percent, so these derived figures are reliable to roughly that. Limitations

  • The geometry behind the near-linear response was my partner's analysis, not mine, so my sizing, FEA, and fabrication are all conditioned on a configuration I did not derive
  • The sizing figures here are calculated minimums, not as-built dimensions. Until the as-built sections sit beside them, the analysis shows what the design needed rather than what it had
  • The 40 MPa allowable is quoted without the material behind it, and without stating whether a factor of safety is already inside that number
  • The 129.7 MPa FEA peak is a mesh artifact. It is named here so that anyone reading the original screenshots knows it was identified and discarded, not quietly relied on
  • The 5.919 buckling factor has no recorded applied load next to it, so it cannot yet be read as a margin against anything
  • No fatigue analysis, which limits what the result says about a device meant to cycle
  • The spring-slack explanation is a diagnosis made after the fact. We did not instrument the spring separately or re-run with the slack removed, so the 13 mm is attributed rather than isolated
  • One article, one run, so nothing here speaks to build-to-build repeatability

What I took from it

The model was accurate about the thing it modelled and silent about the thing that cost the most. Predicted peak force landed within 7% of measurement, which is the part of the analysis I would trust again, while the 13 mm of take-up at the start of the stroke was not an error in a modelled quantity but an unmodelled quantity altogether. Slack, clearance, and preload now go into the model before I spend effort refining numbers that are already close. A 1 N predicted error sitting next to a 33 N measured one is a useful reminder that an optimizer's precision says nothing about its accuracy.
