Motor-Driven Impulse Machine (ME 104)

A 153 g motor-driven lever machine that delivered 1,214 N·s of impulse to two slowly separating surfaces, designed by characterizing the supplied DC motor and holding it at its maximum power point for the full 10-second stroke.

Project overview

The brief framed the machine as a separation launch unit: a habitat and a spent booster stage drift apart slowly, and the machine has one chance to push off the booster to trim the habitat's trajectory. Concretely, it had to apply the largest possible vertical impulse to two surfaces separating at a constant 5 mm/s over a 50 mm stroke, lasting 10 seconds, while weighing as little as possible. A single supplied brushed DC motor and planetary gearbox were the only permitted source of mechanical energy, with no stored strain energy allowed.

Our machine is a three-legged 3D printed frame that stands on the lower platen, a waterjet-cut aluminum lever pivoting near the top of the frame, and a cord wound onto a printed capstan on the gearbox output shaft. The motor reels in the cord, which rotates the lever about its pivot so the wide end bears upward against the pin coupler as the two surfaces separate. The transmission is therefore motor to gearbox to capstan to cord to lever to coupler, with the capstan radius and the lever geometry setting the overall ratio between motor speed and separation speed.

Objective and problem

The separation velocity is fixed by the test, not by the machine. That single fact reshapes the problem: since impulse is the integral of force over time and the surfaces move at a constant 5 mm/s, impulse equals the work done divided by that velocity. Maximizing impulse is therefore identical to maximizing the average mechanical power delivered over the 10 seconds.

So the design target was not raw strength. It was to keep the motor sitting at its maximum power point for the entire stroke, choose a transmission geometry that holds it there as the lever sweeps through its range, and then wrap the lightest possible structure around it that survives the resulting loads.

  • Maximize vertical impulse over a 50 mm stroke at 5 mm/s while minimizing machine mass
  • Use only the supplied Mabuchi RC-260SA-2295 brushed DC motor and Tamiya planetary gearbox; no stored strain energy
  • Stay at or below 2.5 V applied, and keep current low enough that the motor does not overheat across a 10-second run
  • Keep gearbox output torque under the roughly 1 N·m at which the output shaft spring pin is known to shear or buckle
  • Contact the lower platen only on its outer annulus, and never intrude into the region above the pin coupler, even under load-induced deflection
  • No yielding or buckling of the frame, lever, or motor mount under the reaction loads

My role

This was a three-person project. I led the back-of-the-envelope structural analysis, the CAD, the fabrication, and the testing. Motor characterization was done collectively. One teammate owned the kinematic optimization that selected the link lengths and capstan radius; the other owned mass optimization and led material selection, which I contributed to.

Concretely, the free body diagrams of the frame, lever, and motor mount are mine, as is the inverse analysis that sized each member for minimum mass against yielding and buckling. I built the CAD model, printed the frame and capstan, had the lever waterjet cut, assembled the machine, and ran it on the test system.

Engineering process

  1. Requirements. Reduced the brief to a two-term objective — impulse up, mass down — plus a constraint set covering the fixed separation profile, the supplied motor and gearbox, the voltage and thermal limits, the gearbox output torque limit, and the platen contact geometry.
  2. Motor characterization. The supplied spec sheet was known to be inaccurate, so we measured the motor ourselves: winding resistance 0.7193 Ω, torque constant 0.002338 N·m/A, internal friction torque 0.0005845 N·m, and gearbox efficiency 39.16%. Every downstream number rests on these four values, which is why they were measured rather than assumed.
  3. Set point selection. Using the standard DC motor relations T = K·i and V = K·ω + iR with the measured friction torque subtracted, the mechanical power output peaks at 496.19 rad/s and 0.00377 N·m of load torque at 2.5 V. That became the target motor speed, and the gearbox was configured at 400:1.
  4. Kinematic optimization. Owned by a teammate. The mechanism was swept across link lengths, capstan radius, pivot position, and tip radius. For each candidate, the required motor speed was computed across the whole stroke from the cord payout rate, then scored by RMS error against the 496.19 rad/s target, so the winning geometry is the one that holds the motor nearest maximum power for the longest. Candidates that let the lever intrude into the platen's non-contact region were rejected outright.
  5. Structural analysis. Mine. Free body diagrams of the complete frame, the lever alone, and the motor mount in two views, solved for member loads driven by the cord tension and the coupler reaction. The motor mount analysis is what determined how the gearbox had to be constrained rather than simply clamped.
  6. Inverse analysis for mass. Rather than checking a guessed section, I inverted each failure criterion to solve for the smallest section that survives: axial stress σ = F/A, bending σ = My/I, and Euler buckling P = cπ²EI/L². For the lever at 2 mm wide, a maximum moment of 6,812 N·mm against a 72.5 MPa allowable requires 16.79 mm of height.
  7. Material selection. Compared candidates on minimum-mass material indices — ρ/σy for axial loading, ρ/σy2/3 for bending, and ρ/E1/2 for buckling — read against a strength-density chart. Aluminum won for the bending-dominated lever; printed PLA was retained for the compression and buckling members, where it is competitive by index and free to shape.
  8. CAD. Modeled the assembly in Fusion 360: printed tripod legs with thin tension braces, the printed motor and capstan mounts, and the flat aluminum lever profile for waterjet cutting.
  9. FEA. Applied the BOTEA loads — coupler on lever, cord on lever, cord on motor — with constraints at the motor-to-stand, stand-to-platen, and shaft-to-frame and lever interfaces, and checked stress, displacement, and buckling modes for each custom part.
  10. Iteration. Two changes came out of that loop. A large interior cutout was removed from the lever, cutting mass from a part whose bending stress is concentrated at its edges. And a simple support was added on the far side of the capstan, so cord tension is reacted on both sides of the shaft instead of cantilevering it off the gearbox output. FEA was re-run on the final geometry.
  11. Fabrication. Printed the frame and capstan in PLA, had the lever waterjet cut from aluminum plate, and assembled the machine.
  12. Testing. Weighed and geometry-checked the machine, then ran it on the MTS load frame while reaction force was recorded across the 50 mm separation and integrated to give impulse.

Structural analysis and sizing

  • Free body diagrams of the full frame, the lever, and the motor mount in side and top views, solved for member loads from cord tension and the coupler reaction
  • Three failure modes checked on every custom member: axial stress, bending stress, and Euler buckling
  • Inverse analysis solving each criterion for the minimum surviving section at a factor of safety of 2, rather than iterating on guessed geometry
  • Lever sizing: 6,812 N·mm maximum moment, 72.5 MPa allowable from a 145 MPa yield at FoS 2, giving 16.79 mm of required height at 2 mm width
  • Cord tension derived from available output torque and capstan radius, then carried through the lever moment arm to the force delivered at the coupler

Materials and manufacturing

  • Lever: 6061 aluminum plate, waterjet cut, with a large interior cutout for mass reduction [confirm the temper]
  • Frame, motor mount, and capstan: FDM 3D printed PLA
  • Transmission: braided cord wound on the printed capstan
  • Catalog components: supplied Tamiya 72001 motor and planetary gearbox kit, fasteners and shafting

Software and tools

  • Fusion 360 for CAD and finite element analysis
  • Python for the system model, kinematic sweep, and performance plots
  • FDM 3D printing and waterjet cutting for fabrication
  • MTS load frame for force measurement during the separation test

Test procedure

  1. Weigh the machine and check it against the geometric constraints
  2. Install it on the lower platen with the lever bearing on the pin coupler, contact restricted to the permitted annulus
  3. Energize the motor at 2.5 V and allow the brief pre-separation period
  4. Run the 50 mm separation at a constant 5 mm/s for 10 seconds while recording reaction force
  5. Integrate force over time to obtain delivered impulse
  6. Inspect the machine for yielding, buckling, or transmission damage

Results

  • Delivered 1,214 N·s of impulse at 153 g, for 7,935 N·s per kilogram of machine mass, and passed the geometric compliance check
  • That is 86.9% of the 1,397.76 N·s the system model predicted, a 13% shortfall
  • Measured force rose to a 153 N peak at roughly 26 mm of travel and averaged 125 N across the stroke
  • End to end, 32.4% of the motor's mechanical shaft power reached the platen as work. The 39.16% gearbox is the dominant loss, which is why the set point was chosen to maximize shaft power rather than to chase transmission efficiency
  • The tension line broke on the first run. We replaced it with a stronger line and retested immediately, and that second run produced the recorded result — the failure cost us a run, not the project
  • The most likely cause of the shortfall is capstan deflection under cord tension, visible on post-test inspection. Even with the added simple support, the printed capstan and its shaft flexed enough to change the effective radius and add friction, both of which pull delivered impulse down. This is a post-test hypothesis, not an instrumented root cause
  • No yielding or buckling of the frame, lever, or mounts was observed after testing

Limitations

  • The gap between predicted and measured impulse was never instrumented. Capstan deflection is the best explanation from visual inspection, but cord stretch, wrap slip, and error in the measured gearbox efficiency were not separated out
  • Gearbox efficiency was characterized as a single number and treated as constant across the speed range, when in reality it varies with load and speed
  • One successful run, so there is no measure of run-to-run variance
  • The selected link lengths, capstan radius, and pivot coordinates were not recorded after the project closed out, so the geometry cannot be reproduced from this write-up alone

What I took from it

The useful insight came early and was almost entirely about problem framing: because the separation speed was fixed, impulse reduced to average power, and the whole design became an exercise in parking the motor at its power peak and building the lightest structure that could stand it. Everything else followed from that. The lesson from test day was the opposite in character — the 13% we lost was not in the analysis but in a printed part flexing under load, which no free body diagram was ever going to catch. Next time I would put a stiffer or metal capstan on the critical torque path from the start.
