Toolbox and Hammer: Six Fabrication Processes (ME 103)

The ME 103 structured lab artifacts: a cast and machined hammer with a turned steel handle, and a folded, riveted, and welded sheet metal toolbox — both built to drawing and graded against a written defect list.

Project overview

The first five weeks of ME 103 are structured labs in the Stanford Product Realization Lab, covering the shop's core processes: milling, turning, casting, sheet metal forming, welding, and finishing. Every student builds the same two artifacts from the same drawings — a hammer and a toolbox — so unlike the rest of the projects here, this one is not about design. It is entirely about execution against a print.

That makes it the clearest evidence on this site of hands-on process breadth. In six weeks I cast and machined a hammer head, turned a threaded steel handle and a pair of plastic faces, formed and hemmed a sheet metal toolbox, joined it three different ways, and turned a wooden carry handle on the wood lathe for extra credit.

Objective and acceptance criteria

What makes this exercise useful is that it is graded like an inspection, not like a design review. The rubric is a published list of specific defects with point values attached, so the goal is not to make something interesting — it is to hit the drawing and leave nothing for an inspector to find.

  • Toolbox: folds located correctly, holes aligned, all corners completed correctly, hems complete, straight, and folded flush, and every burr and sharp edge removed
  • Welded corner: no blowout holes, and no grinding of the corner at all — grinding is itself a two-point deduction, so the weld is judged as-welded with no rework path available
  • Hammer head: gate vestige and casting flash completely removed, a consistent and intentional surface finish, centered holes, and geometry matching the drawing
  • Turned steel handle: length to drawing, thread centered and clean, end chamfered, and the whole handle free of chatter marks and divots from the lathe chuck
  • Faces: made to print, secure, straight, and seated sub-surface in the head per the drawing
  • Assembly: the handle threads on easily and sits flush and straight to the head
  • Due at the end of week five with no late submissions accepted, and worth 25% of the course grade

My role

Solo. I performed every operation on both artifacts, plus the extra credit wooden handle.

Fabrication process

  1. Casting. Poured the silicon bronze hammer head, then removed the gate vestige and all casting flash and worked the surface to a consistent, intentional finish, including the round area where the head mates to the faces and the flat opposite the nameplate.
  2. Milling. Faced the cast head to establish flat reference surfaces, then drilled and tapped it for the handle and for retaining the faces, with the holes held centered to the drawing.
  3. Metal turning. Turned the steel handle to drawing length with a centered external thread and a chamfered end. Workholding mattered as much as cutting here, since chuck divots and chatter marks are both graded defects and both come from how the part is held rather than how it is cut.
  4. Plastic turning. Turned the two hammer faces and fitted them into the head so they sit sub-surface, secure, and straight.
  5. Sheet metal forming. Cut, bent, and hemmed the toolbox body, locating the folds so the holes would line up at assembly and the hems would close flush.
  6. Joining. Assembled the toolbox using three methods on one part: rivets, spot welds, and oxyacetylene welded corners. The corners were the highest-stakes operation on either artifact, because the welds had to come out clean enough to submit without being dressed.
  7. Finishing and deburring. Removed burrs and sharp edges throughout, with no additional surface finishing applied, per the drawing.
  8. Wood turning (extra credit). Turned a custom wooden carry handle for the toolbox on the wood lathe, completed as an optional extra credit project in week six.

Notes on the harder operations

  • The oxyacetylene corners are a single-shot operation. Blowout holes cost points and grinding the corner costs more, so the only acceptable outcome is a weld that is right the first time at the correct heat and travel speed
  • Casting cleanup is judged on completeness and consistency rather than on how shiny it is — flash and gate vestige fully gone, and the same intentional finish across the whole part
  • On the turned handle, the defects called out in the rubric come from workholding and setup rather than from the cut, which is a useful thing to learn early
  • Hems have to be straight and closed flush, which depends on getting the flat pattern and bend locations right before any metal moves

Results

  • Both artifacts completed and submitted within the week-five deadline, which had no late allowance
  • Six distinct processes demonstrated on two parts: casting, milling, turning, sheet metal forming, welding, and finishing — across metal, plastic, and wood
  • Three joining methods executed on the toolbox alone: riveting, spot welding, and oxyacetylene welding
  • Welded corners passed as-welded, with no grinding or dressing used to hide the joint
  • Wooden carry handle turned and completed for extra credit in week six
  • The hammer went on to earn a second life as the test coupon for the patina trial on my ME 103 final project, since it was the only piece of matching material I had to experiment on

What I took from it

Building to someone else's drawing against a published defect list is a different skill from designing your own part, and it is the one most directly relevant to shop and manufacturing work. The habit it builds is inspecting your own output against written criteria before handing it over, rather than hoping it passes. The no-grinding rule on the welded corner made that concrete: when there is no rework path, the only option is to get the process parameters right before you start.
