Miniature Tesla Coil from Salvaged Electronics

A battery-powered miniature Tesla coil built around a high-voltage circuit salvaged from a bug-zapper racket, with a hand-wound secondary on PVC pipe and a chassis of acrylic and hardware-store parts. Entirely self-taught, with no coursework behind it.

Project overview

A Tesla coil is a resonant air-core transformer. A driver circuit pushes pulses of current through a primary winding of a few turns, that primary couples magnetically into a secondary of many hundreds of turns, and the resonance between the secondary's inductance and the capacitance of the electrode on top of it multiplies the voltage far beyond what the turns ratio alone would give. The result is a high-frequency, high-voltage output that produces visible discharge and can light a fluorescent tube held nearby with no electrical connection at all.

I built a miniature one on a desktop scale. The high-voltage driver is the circuit board out of an electric fly-swatter racket, which already contains a battery-powered boost converter and a small step-up transformer, making it the cheapest high-voltage supply available. The secondary is enameled magnet wire wound by hand onto PVC pipe, the topload is a brass drawer knob, and the chassis is two acrylic plates on nylon standoffs. Everything else came from a hardware store.

This was a self-directed learning project rather than an original design. No class I had taken covered high-voltage or resonant circuit behaviour, and reading about it was not the same as watching a coil come up on the bench. I worked from a published build as my baseline, which is credited in the reference below.

Objective

The goal was understanding, and the deliverable was a working coil. I wanted to get to the point where high-voltage behaviour was something I had handled rather than something I had read about — how a resonant circuit behaves, why winding consistency matters, what a topload actually does, and what it feels like to work around a live high-voltage output.

  • Produce visible high-frequency high-voltage output from a battery supply
  • Build it entirely from salvaged and hardware-store parts
  • Keep it small enough to handle, transport, and demonstrate on a table
  • Package it so that the high-voltage sections are insulated from anything a person touches, and so the structure itself provides no conductive path around the coil

My role

Solo, and self-taught. I sourced and salvaged the parts, reworked the driver board, wound the secondary, built the chassis, assembled it, and brought it up.

Build process

  1. Reference study. Worked through a published build to understand the architecture before buying anything, so I knew what each part was for rather than following a parts list.
  2. Driver salvage. A bug-zapper racket is a battery-powered high-voltage supply that happens to be sold as a household product. I opened one, removed the board, desoldered its original output connections, and soldered in my own leads so I could drive the coil with it.
  3. Secondary winding. Wound enameled magnet wire onto a PVC form using a winding jig, with electrical tape bands marking the start and end of the winding. The winding has to stay single-layer and tightly packed: gaps, overlaps, and crossed turns all change the secondary's inductance, which moves the frequency the coil resonates at and detunes it from the driver.
  4. Topload. Fitted a brass drawer knob at the top of the secondary. The topload sets the capacitance at the high-voltage end, which together with the secondary inductance determines the resonant frequency, and its smooth rounded surface gives the discharge a controlled place to leave from instead of concentrating at a wire end.
  5. Base circuit. Assembled the driver, a bank of high-voltage components, and an adjustable gap formed between two brackets on threaded brass screws onto the lower acrylic plate, with the primary connected to the coil at the base. [Confirm the topology here — whether this is a spark-gap circuit with a capacitor bank, and what the component values are.]
  6. Chassis. Built the frame from two acrylic plates separated by nylon standoffs, with brass hardware. The material choice was deliberate rather than aesthetic: acrylic and nylon are both insulators, so the structure holding the coil does not offer a path around it.
  7. Bring-up and adjustment. Powered it up and adjusted until the output was stable and repeatable rather than intermittent.
  8. Demonstration. Verified output by holding a compact fluorescent bulb near the topload and watching it light with nothing connected to it. Handled the coil only when unpowered, kept it away from other electronics, and kept a working distance from the energized topload.

What I learned about the physics by building it

  • The voltage gain is not just the turns ratio. It comes from resonance, which is why the coil only works properly when the secondary and topload resonate near the frequency the driver is producing
  • Winding quality is an electrical parameter, not a cosmetic one. An uneven secondary is a differently tuned secondary
  • The topload is a capacitor, and changing it retunes the whole coil
  • Output at these frequencies couples through air well enough to excite a gas in a nearby tube, which is why the fluorescent bulb lights without contact

Materials and construction

  • Salvaged: the complete high-voltage driver board from a consumer bug-zapper racket
  • Hardware store: PVC pipe and end caps, brass drawer knob, acrylic sheet, nylon standoffs, brass and steel fasteners
  • Purchased: enameled magnet wire and a winding jig
  • Insulating materials chosen throughout the load-bearing structure

Tools

  • Soldering iron and desoldering for the driver rework
  • Coil winding jig
  • Hand tools for the acrylic chassis

Reference

Design baseline: this published mini Tesla coil build. The architecture and part selection follow it; the construction, winding, chassis, and assembly are mine.

Results

  • The coil works. It produces stable high-frequency high-voltage output from a battery supply and lights a compact fluorescent bulb held near the topload with no connection to it, which is the clearest available demonstration that the output is both high voltage and high frequency
  • I taught myself an area no course had covered, by building rather than reading, and came out able to reason about resonance, coupling, and topload capacitance as physical things rather than equations
  • Repurposed a consumer product's circuit for a completely different function, which required understanding what the board actually did well enough to rewire its output
  • Built a package where the material choices serve the electrical requirement: insulating chassis, insulating standoffs, and an adjustable gap rather than a fixed one

What this project is not

  • It is a reference build, not an original design. The architecture came from a published project and I have said so
  • I did no analysis. I never calculated the expected resonant frequency from the secondary inductance and topload capacitance, and never measured the actual frequency
  • I did not record the turn count or wire gauge, which means I cannot reproduce my own coil from my own documentation — the single most useful lesson to come out of it
  • Output was verified qualitatively, by the fluorescent bulb and by visible discharge, with no measurement of arc length, current draw, or power

What I would do to finish it properly

There is an afternoon of work that would turn this from a build into an experiment: count the turns and measure the wire gauge, calculate the predicted resonant frequency from the secondary inductance and topload capacitance, then measure the actual frequency with an oscilloscope and a small pickup coil and compare the two. Add current draw at the battery and a measured arc length, and the page would have numbers in it instead of adjectives. I know how to do that now in a way I did not when I built it, which is itself the point of having built it.
