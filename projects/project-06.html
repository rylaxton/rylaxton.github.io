GROOVA: Hand-Cranked Turntable Mechanism (ME 102)

A hand-cranked turntable built around an 1868 mechanism for drill feed motion, developed through three prototypes each with a written driver question and success metrics before it was built.

Project overview

The assignment was to take an assigned mechanism from Henry T. Brown's 507 Mechanical Movements, invent a use case for it, and then argue that the mechanism was the right choice for that use. We drew movement 99: a spiral guide on the face of a disk, catalogued in 1868 as a way to generate the feed motion of a drilling machine. Rotate the disk and a follower riding the spiral is carried steadily inward or outward, turning one rotation into a controlled radial translation.

Researching it, we noticed the same kinematics in something far more familiar than a drill press: a record player. A stylus tracking a spiral groove is exactly this mechanism, with the groove doing the guiding and the tonearm doing the translating. So we built a hand-cranked turntable — a crank drives a bevel gear pair into a vertical spindle, the spindle turns a grooved platter, and a horn-and-needle arm follows the groove inward as it rotates. We called it GROOVA, for Guided Rotational Oscillation of Vinyl Assembly.

Objective and constraints

The engineering content of this project sat in the prototyping method rather than in analysis. We had three required prototypes, a tight budget, a fixed envelope, and a final assessment on function rather than appearance, which meant the useful discipline was deciding in advance what each prototype was supposed to answer.

  • Demonstrate a working use case for the assigned mechanism and justify why it suits that use better than the alternatives
  • Build at least three prototypes, escalating from rough to fully functional
  • Fit the finished mechanism inside an 8 × 8 × 8 in volume
  • Work within $20 per student
  • Be graded on functionality, reliability, robustness, and finishing — so the needle has to stay in the groove repeatedly, not once
  • Produce a full engineering drawing package, including a dimensioned critical part with tolerances, from a properly assembled CAD model

My role

A two-person project. We both worked across the whole thing rather than splitting it by discipline — research, CAD, printing, laser cutting, tolerance testing, assembly, and the presentation were all shared. My partner led the engineering drawing package, including the exploded view. The critical part we selected for the dimensioned drawing was the track and needle, since that interface is what the entire mechanism depends on.

Prototyping method

Each prototype was given a driver question and a set of pass criteria in writing before it was built. That is the part of this project I would carry into professional work: it forces you to decide what you are actually trying to learn, and it stops a prototype from becoming a smaller version of the final thing built for no particular reason.

  1. Prototype 1 — is the motion method feasible? The driver questions were how rotational motion could translate a device linearly, and how to fix an object in a track while still letting it move freely along it. Pass criteria: all parts print successfully, the motion method demonstrates promise, and all the key geometries and relationships are visible. Built as a printed enclosure with a spiral grooved platter.
  2. Prototype 2 — can we make it faster without losing fit? Two driver questions: whether laser cutting would cut fabrication time while still giving acceptable fit, and whether a needle on the tonearm could follow the groove reliably. Pass criteria: the structure holds alignment after adhesive, and the needle stays in the groove through a full rotation. Built from laser-cut flat stock with a spiral disc.
  3. Tolerance characterization. Between prototypes we printed deliberate test coupons rather than guessing at fits: a set of threaded samples to find what thread geometry actually works in PLA, and a set of shaft samples to match shaft diameters to hole diameters. Printed threads and printed press fits both depend on process behaviour that no CAD dimension tells you, so the only way to get it right is to print a sweep and measure what fits.
  4. Prototype 3 — is it reliable and serviceable? Driver questions moved from "does it work" to "does it keep working": whether fasteners would improve reassembly, whether the funnel and needle structure would reliably stay in the track, and whether user input stayed uninterrupted. Pass criteria: assembly and disassembly possible without deforming parts, the needle stays in the groove through a full rotation, and the needle completes a full rotation without sticking or falling out.
  5. Final build. Rebuilt with fasteners replacing adhesive, gears positively located rather than floating, and the track cleared of the blockages that had been stalling the needle. Eight printed parts and eight laser-cut parts in PLA and acrylic.
  6. Drawing package and presentation. Assembled and joined the full CAD model, then produced the drawing package from it, and presented the mechanism with a photo essay and a live demonstration.

Design decisions and why

  • Bevel gears to take the crank axis through ninety degrees into the spindle, so the user cranks from the side of a box rather than reaching over the platter
  • A funnel-and-needle follower rather than a plain pin, giving the needle a stable seat in the groove and the object a recognizable gramophone form
  • Fasteners over adhesive in the final build, because the earlier bonded assemblies could not be taken apart for adjustment without damage — serviceability was a stated pass criterion, not an afterthought
  • Printed threads instead of captive nuts where space was tight, which is what drove the thread tolerance testing
  • Acrylic for flat structure and PLA for anything with three-dimensional form, splitting the work between laser cutting for speed and printing for geometry

Failure modes found and fixed

  • Needle stalling on blockages in the groove track, fixed by cleaning up the track geometry
  • Parts not fitting or requiring adhesive to hold position, fixed by characterizing printed fits and moving to fasteners
  • Gears not fully constrained in position, fixed by positively locating them in the final build

Tools

  • CAD for the assembly and the drawing package
  • FDM 3D printing and laser cutting
  • Hand assembly and fastening

Results

  • The mechanism works and meets every pass criterion we wrote for it. The needle tracks the groove through a full rotation without sticking or falling out, user input is uninterrupted through the crank, and the assembly can be taken apart and put back together without deforming parts
  • Three prototypes plus tolerance coupons, each with its question answered before the next one started
  • Printed thread and shaft-to-hole fits were established empirically rather than assumed, which is what allowed the final build to drop adhesive entirely
  • A complete engineering drawing package produced from a fully assembled CAD model, with the track-and-needle interface as the dimensioned critical part

What we took from it

  • Don't chase a boondoggle. We repeatedly sank time into minor components that later got removed. The fix for the next project was to assess whether a part is necessary before detailing it, and to lay out a critical path to a result rather than working on whatever was in front of us
  • Manufacturing time is a design input. Long print and cut queues set the pace of the whole project, so designing for fast processes is not an optimization — it is what determines how many iterations you get
  • Design for assembly from the start. Joint connections, thread sizes, and the order of operations all have to be decided as part of the design, not discovered at assembly. A mechanism that cannot be reassembled cannot be maintained

Limitations

  • We never recorded the quantitative results of the tolerance testing. The method was right and the fits worked, but the clearances that came out of it are lost, which means the useful part of that work cannot be reused — a documentation failure, not a technical one
  • The gear ratio, groove pitch, and needle travel distance were never calculated or recorded, so the mechanism's motion cannot be predicted from this write-up
  • The required force analysis was done late in the project and I would not stand behind the numbers in it. It satisfied the deliverable without informing the design
  • Reliability was demonstrated over a handful of rotations by hand, not over any meaningful cycle count
