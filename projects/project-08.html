Walnut and Bronze Key Shelf (ME 103)

A wall-mounted key shelf in live-edge walnut, with lathe-turned phosphor bronze knobs and sand-cast silicon bronze brackets — a product realization project spanning turning, foundry, milling, woodworking, and finishing.

Project overview

ME 103, Product Realization: Design and Making, is built around the Stanford Product Realization Lab. Students take structured labs in the core fabrication processes — turning, milling, metal forming and joining, and foundry — and then design and build a final project of their own. The requirement that shapes everything is that the project must use at least two of those core processes substantively. Waterjet cutting, woodworking, and finishing do not count, and using a core process lightly, such as drilling a hole on the mill, is explicitly insufficient.

I designed a wall-mounted key holder with a shelf: a live-edge walnut backboard and shelf, three turned phosphor bronze knobs for keys, and two sand-cast silicon bronze brackets carrying the shelf, patinated to an aged finish. The personal brief was straightforward — my partner and I had just moved in together, and I wanted something for the new place that we would actually use every day.

Objective and problem

The real design problem was upstream of the object itself: choose something where two core processes have a genuine reason to exist. It is easy to design a nice piece of furniture and then bolt on a turned part to satisfy a requirement. I wanted the opposite, a piece where the knobs had to be turned and the brackets had to be cast, because that is what the forms and materials called for.

Turning earned its place through the knobs: identical repeated parts carrying a taper and an external thread. Casting earned its place through the brackets: a sculptural profile with curves that would be impractical to machine, in a material that takes a patina well.

  • Use at least two core processes substantively, with a completed pattern and a cast part integrated into the final object
  • Produce visually identical knobs, since asymmetry between them would be obvious on an object mounted at eye level
  • Carry the shelf load through the brackets and a joint in the backboard
  • Work within the course budget ceiling of $100 out of pocket plus up to $100 in scavenged material value
  • Fit the quarter's schedule and the lab's available shop time
  • Make three materials — walnut, phosphor bronze, silicon bronze — read as one coherent object

My role

Solo project. I did the design, all pattern work, all machining, the casting, finishing, woodworking, and assembly.

Engineering process

  1. Concept and scoping. Worked from a wide idea list down to a short list and then to an approved project, testing each candidate against whether two core processes had a real role in it rather than a decorative one.
  2. Bracket design, four iterations. I wanted the brackets to read as thick tree branches. I modeled that in CAD first and could not get the form to look natural. I then carved a branch pattern by hand from machinable modeling board, which took a long time and looked no better than the CAD version. At that point I dropped the organic direction entirely and moved to a Victorian bracket profile, back in CAD. That was the right call and produced a far better result — it just arrived later in the quarter than it should have.
  3. Turning. Turned the knobs from scavenged phosphor bronze stock to roughly 0.5 in diameter, each with a taper and a 1/4-20 external thread, working to a written operation sequence so the parts would come out matching. Phosphor bronze proved difficult: its high thermal conductivity meant the work heated quickly and turned gummy, which fought both the cut and the finish.
  4. Inspection and rejection. Four knobs were turned and three were installed. The first one came out too long to match the others, so it was set aside rather than fitted.
  5. Pattern and sand casting. Mounted the final bracket pattern to a fixed pattern board and sand cast the brackets in silicon bronze. Gates were removed and the surfaces filed and sanded to clean up unevenness from the mold.
  6. Milling. Faced the back and top surfaces of each casting to create flat, square mounting datums against the backboard and the underside of the shelf, then drilled and tapped those faces so the brackets could be bolted rather than glued in place.
  7. Finish qualification. Before touching the brackets, I ran the liver of sulfur patina on the hammer from the structured lab, which was the only piece of matching material I had available to test on. Immersion time was the single variable. Two to three minutes went far too dark, so the brackets went in for roughly 30 seconds.
  8. Woodworking. Cut the backboard and shelf from live-edge walnut slabs at 1 in thick, with the backboard about 16 in wide. The shelf seats into a dado in the backboard, which I cut on the table saw in small increments, test-fitting between passes until the joint was a friction fit rather than a glue-dependent one.
  9. Assembly and finishing. Glued the shelf into the dado, bolted the brackets through their tapped faces, installed the knobs, and finished the wood with Tried and True linseed oil.

Finishing

  • Linseed oil on the walnut, chosen to deepen the dark grain against the warm bronze

Results

  • Completed and installed; the shelf holds daily items and the knobs hold keys, which was the functional requirement
  • Both core process requirements met substantively: turning with a taper, an external thread, and repeated identical parts; foundry with a completed pattern and cast parts integrated into the finished object
  • Three of four turned knobs matched closely enough to install. The first was rejected for being too long — caught by inspection before assembly rather than after
  • The patina trial saved the brackets. Testing on a scrap coupon showed 2–3 minutes produced a far darker finish than intended, so the real parts went in for about 30 seconds and came out at the tone I wanted on the first attempt
  • The dado reached a friction fit through incremental test cutting, so the shelf locates itself rather than depending on glue for position
  • Two defects in the finished piece, both minor: bark broke away from the live edge during glue-up of the shelf into the dado, and one knob has a visible step where the taper meets the flat it was supposed to blend into, caused by a tooling error on the manual lathe

What I would do differently

  • I spent two full design attempts, one in CAD and one carving modeling board, trying to make an organic branch form work before abandoning it. A quick low-fidelity mockup would have told me the same thing in a fraction of the time, and would have left more schedule for the casting
  • The bark on a live edge is fragile and the glue-up is exactly where it gets handled and clamped. I would stabilize or protect it before assembly rather than after
  • The patina experiment varied only immersion time. Dilution and solution temperature also matter, and holding them constant by default rather than by decision means I do not actually know how sensitive the result was

What I took from it

The habit worth keeping from this project is qualifying a process on scrap before committing the real part. The patina test cost me a few minutes on a hammer I had already made and it is the reason the brackets came out right the first time. The other lesson is about material behavior driving process: phosphor bronze machines badly because it sheds heat into the tool and the work, and no amount of feed and speed adjustment fully solved it. Knowing that going in would have changed how I planned the knob operations.
