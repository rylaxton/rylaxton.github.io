300 lbf Liquid Engine Thrust Stand

A welded steel thrust stand for a 300 lbf liquid bipropellant rocket engine, taken from requirements through trade studies, hand analysis, FEA, and a scaled prototype to a fabricated structure that has since supported six to eight cold-flow test campaigns.

Project overview

Cardinal Propulsion Lab is developing a liquid bipropellant, regeneratively cooled rocket engine rated to 300 lbf with a nine-second burn, roughly a foot long and 5.5 in at maximum diameter. Testing it requires ground support hardware that holds the engine fixed against its own thrust, keeps people and hardware safe through repeated firings, and lets thrust be measured accurately enough to be worth collecting.

I led the design of that thrust stand as Test and Ground Support Equipment Co-Lead. The result is a horizontal welded structure of A500 steel square tube with A36 plate at each end: the engine's injector manifold bolts to three load cells, the load cells bolt to a front thrust plate, and a rear plate bolts to a structural I-beam so thrust reacts into the building rather than into ballast. It was fabricated in early 2026 and has been in service since.

The ground electronics, sensor selection, and data acquisition for this test setup are covered separately in my Cardinal Propulsion Lab electrical ground system project.

Objective and requirements

The governing requirement was structural: survive the engine's thrust with a factor of safety of 3 against yield and 5 against ultimate, because a failure during a live combustion test injures people and destroys test articles. The secondary question was whether the structure would deform enough under load to corrupt the thrust measurement — a stand that survives but flexes gives you bad data and can compress the propellant feedline.

Those sat alongside a set of practical requirements that mattered just as much for a student team sharing a test site.

My role

Test and Ground Support Equipment Co-Lead. I owned the thrust stand design from start to finish: requirements, the configuration and material trade studies, all CAD iterations, the hand calculations, and the structural FEA. I also ran the design through ME 80 as a course project, where a teammate independently reproduced the FEA and a third built and tested the scaled wooden prototype, giving me a second check on work I had already done for the team.

Engineering process

  1. Research and requirements. Surveyed thrust stands built by other university rocket teams, then wrote requirements specific to our engine, test site, and team, each with a stated purpose and a performance metric rather than a vague goal.
  2. Configuration trade. Scored horizontal against vertical on complexity, cost, engine access, proneness to specific failure modes, and size. Horizontal won: it fires directly into the frame so members carry compression rather than tension, it needs no flame diverter, it has no gravity tare, and it is substantially cheaper. The cost is that it does not represent flight orientation and can trap propellant.
  3. Joining trade. Scored welding against bolted hardware on complexity, cost, load-bearing predictability, and strength. Welding won on cost and strength and on being able to join faces of differing geometry in one operation; its weakness is that weld quality is hard to verify after the fact.
  4. Material trade. Scored A500 steel against 6061-T6 aluminum on weight, strength, stiffness, cost, and machinability, cross-referenced against an Ashby modulus-versus-cost-per-volume plot. Steel won on strength, stiffness, and cost; aluminum's advantage was purely mass, at 2.9 times lower density but 1.5 times the price per length.
  5. Design iteration. The first CAD was heavily over-built, with X-trusses on both sides and the top plus two forward diagonals. The second removed most of that redundancy and introduced the three-hole tripod load cell pattern, so that any non-axial thrust component shows up as a difference between the three readings instead of disappearing into a single-axis measurement. The third removed the front legs: I had added them for ground clearance and anti-tipping before working out that the engine's weight produces a negligible moment against the center of gravity of a steel structure this heavy.
  6. Plate analysis. Plate bending under a load distributed over a small central area is not a case covered in statics or mechanics of materials, and the closed-form routes I found mostly required finite element methods. I used Roark's Formulas for Stress and Strain, Table 11.4 case 1b, treating the plate as simply supported on all four edges with the load applied over the circle containing the three load cells.
  7. Frame analysis. For the members, I assumed a rigid front plate and rigid upper verticals with the thrust distributed, set the z-direction displacements equal using d = FL/EA, and solved the resulting system with a moment balance for the member loads.
  8. FEA. Fixed the rear plate to represent bolting into the I-beam and applied 300 lbf, checking von Mises stress and displacement. Ran a second case with the front plate removed to isolate its effect on the horizontal members.
  9. Scaled prototype. Built a three-quarter-scale wooden version, driven by the fact that nominal 2×2 lumber is actually 1.5 in square. Plates were laser cut from 1/4 in birch plywood and the assembly was bonded with wood glue.
  10. Load testing. Loaded the prototype vertically with incrementally increasing weight, then ran a separate plate deflection test on a thinner coupon when the full prototype proved too stiff to deflect measurably.
  11. Fabrication. Ordered the steel and had the structure welded by a professional welding company under an arrangement with the team, so cost was materials only.
  12. Service. Installed in the team workspace and used for cold-flow test campaigns on the engine.

The member calculations were run on a 0.9024 in² section, equivalent to a 0.120 in wall, while the structure was built from 3/16 in wall at 1.36 in². The as-built frame therefore carries about 1.5 times the section assumed in analysis, making the reported margins conservative.

Analysis methods and assumptions

  • Thrust plate: σmax = (3W/2πt²)[(1+ν)ln(2b/πr) + β], simply supported on all four edges, load distributed over the 2.5 in radius circle through the load cell positions, β interpolated from the Roark's table
  • Frame: rigid front plate and rigid upper verticals, distributed 300 lbf, equal z-displacements solved with d = FL/EA plus a moment balance
  • Neither hand model captured plate slots or bolted connections at the I-beam interface, and welds were not modeled in FEA
  • Keeping most members the same length was a deliberate choice that made the displacement and symmetry assumptions defensible

Failure modes considered

  • Weld joint failure, leading to overall structural collapse; mitigated by orienting welds normal to load and specifying generous weld thickness margin
  • Thrust plate deflection, causing incorrect load cell readings and feedline compression; mitigated with thicker plate and supporting diagonals
  • Rear plate deflection, causing feedline compression and localized stress; mitigated by thicker plate and minimizing the area mismatch between plate and I-beam section
  • Engine linear and angular displacement, producing non-axial thrust readings and load cell damage; mitigated by supporting the engine at three points
  • Fatigue from repeated firings was identified but not tested, on the judgment that the structure will not see enough cycles for it to govern

Prototype and test

  • Three-quarter-scale wooden prototype: nominal 2×2 lumber at an actual 1.5 in square set the scale factor; plates laser cut from 1/4 in birch plywood, which scaled to 66.7% rather than 75% because of stock availability
  • Wood selected for rapid prototyping using a strength versus relative cost per unit volume Ashby plot, accepting that yield strength, modulus, and ductility all differ substantially from steel
  • Loaded vertically with incrementally increasing weight to roughly 250 lbf with no yielding, no frame failure, and no observable plate deflection — team members stood on the structure without breaking it
  • Plate deflection measured separately on a 1/8 in birch coupon of larger area at the same length-to-width ratio, photographed before and after loading, with angle change extracted by pixel measurement in Fiji and converted to deflection through δ = 7.25·sinθ
  • Seven load points from 0 to 24.25 lbf produced deflections from 0 to 0.238 in

Software and tools

  • CAD and FEA in Autodesk Fusion
  • MATLAB for the plate stress calculation
  • Roark's Formulas for Stress and Strain for the plate bending case
  • Ashby material selection charts; laser cutting and wood shop for the prototype
  • Fiji image analysis for deflection measurement

Results

  • The stand was built and is in service. It has supported six to eight cold-flow campaigns on the engine, used to measure injector discharge coefficients so that hot-fire performance can be predicted ahead of a live test. Its value there is that it holds the engine in the same orientation it will occupy during a hot fire, which a simpler fixture would not
  • Both structural questions were answered affirmatively: the design clears the required FoS of 3 to yield and 5 to ultimate by a wide margin, and predicted deflection of 4.5 × 10⁻⁴ in is far too small to bias the thrust measurement
  • The hand calculation and the FEA agreed on frame factor of safety to within 0.7%, 98.1 against 97.5, which is the strongest validation result in the project — two independent methods on the same structure
  • The thrust plate is correctly identified as the limiting component, at 17.9 to yield against the frame's 98, and the FEA agreed that the front plate and the left-side verticals are where bending concentrates
  • Fabrication cost was materials only, since welding was donated under a team arrangement
  • It has never been used for a hot fire. At 70 to 80 lb it is too heavy and bulky to move to the hot-fire site conveniently, and the team uses smaller stands there instead

What the mass outcome actually taught me

A factor of safety approaching 100 on the frame is not a good result. It is proof of over-design, and the mass that came with it is exactly why the stand does not get taken to hot fire. Mobility was written into the requirements from the start — fit in a pickup, carried by two people — but in the trade studies I weighted strength and cost above it, and the structure I ended up with satisfies the requirement on paper while being annoying enough in practice that people reach for something else.

The right answer for the next iteration is a smaller stand with a margin actually near the specified 3 and 5, sized for transport rather than sized for certainty. That conclusion came from the hardware being used, not from any analysis I ran, and it is the most useful thing this project produced.

Limitations

  • Welds were never modeled in FEA, and weld quality on the as-built structure has not been inspected beyond visual check — the joining trade identified this as welding's central weakness and the project did not close it
  • The prototype could not be loaded to failure, so the test confirmed only that the structure comfortably survives 250 lbf, not where it actually breaks
  • The plate deflection data is noisy and not monotonic, with too few points to establish a relationship, and the absolute deflections are far larger than the FEA because the coupon was thinner, larger, and lower in modulus. It demonstrates the right trend and nothing more
  • Hand models assumed rigid plates and rigid verticals, which the FEA showed to be wrong — both bend. Relaxing those assumptions is the obvious next step for the analysis
  • Fatigue and thermal effects on the welds were identified but not analyzed or tested
