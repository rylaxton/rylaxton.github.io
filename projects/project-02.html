SkyCool Systems PVC Jacketing Spring Clip

An extrudable plastic spring clip that retains jacketing on 2.5 in nominal PVC pipe, sized from a closed-form bending model and a MATLAB design sweep, then validated in FEA and across six printed prototypes.

Project overview

Outdoor PVC piping at SkyCool Systems is wrapped in a protective jacketing that has to be held against the pipe by a clip. The clip sits outside for years, so it has to resist UV and corrosion, retain the jacketing without loosening, and be cheap enough to produce in quantity. The part is a C-shaped spring clip that is spread by hand, passed over the pipe outer diameter, and allowed to snap closed around it.

I designed the clip as a constant rectangular cross section so it could be produced by extrusion, which keeps the per-part cost low and scales to volume. Plastic was chosen over metal deliberately: the clip has to store a large installation deflection without yielding, and a lower elastic modulus does exactly that at a given geometry, while also removing any corrosion concern.

Objective and problem

The clip has to spread far enough to clear a 2.5 in outer diameter without yielding, yet close with enough force to hold the jacketing in place. Those two goals pull against each other: the clip behaves as a spring, so every additional inch of installation deflection raises both the installation force and the peak bending stress. The design problem was to find the geometry and material that satisfy both ends of that trade with margin against yield.

Two levers were available. Widening the relaxed throat opening reduces the deflection each arm has to travel to clear the pipe. Selecting a lower-modulus material reduces the stress produced by whatever deflection remains.

  • Pass over a 2.5 in nominal PVC outer diameter with a factor of safety of at least 2.0 against yield in bending
  • Retain the jacketing against the pipe over a multi-year outdoor service life
  • Resist UV exposure and corrosion
  • Hold a constant cross section so the part can be extruded rather than molded
  • Install and remove by hand, without tools
  • Keep material and manufacturing cost low at production quantity

My role

This was a solo project during my internship. I owned it end to end: defining the requirements from the application constraints, developing the geometry, deriving the bending and statics model by hand, writing the MATLAB script that sized the part, building the CAD and running the FEA in Fusion 360, printing the prototypes, and running the installation testing on the actual pipe and jacketing.

Engineering process

  1. Requirements. Translated the application constraints — UV and corrosion resistance, multi-year outdoor life, jacketing retention, low cost, fast to manufacture — into a mechanical requirement: survive the installation deflection over a 2.5 in outer diameter with a factor of safety of 2.0 against yield.
  2. Concept development. Identified installation deflection as the governing driver of both force and stress, and targeted it directly by widening the relaxed throat opening and selecting a low-modulus material. Added rounded tips so the clip can be pried off by hand when the jacketing needs to come away.
  3. Statics. Worked a force and moment balance on the clip. Symmetry gives equal loads at the two tips and a crown reaction of twice the tip load, so the problem reduces to a single arm carrying a spring force of the form F = kδ.
  4. Stress model. Treated each half of the clip as a cantilever beam deflected far enough for the tip to clear the pipe diameter. Bending is the governing failure mode, so the design constraint becomes σ = My/I with I = lt³/12 and y = t/2 for the rectangular section.
  5. Closed-form sizing. Combined the beam model with the clip geometry, taking the effective arm length as the arc length from tip to crown rather than a straight lever arm, which is the more conservative and more physically realistic choice. That collapses to a single sizing relation, t = 2·Sy·L² / (3·E·δ·FoS), relating wall thickness directly to throat opening for a given material and factor of safety.
  6. MATLAB design sweep. Swept 200 relaxed throat openings from 0.25 to 0.75 of the pipe diameter. For each, the script computes required deflection per arm, stress-limited thickness, arm stiffness, installation force, installation stress, and the resulting factor of safety, then flags the openings that stay under the allowable stress.
  7. CAD. Modeled the selected geometry in Fusion 360 with a constant rectangular cross section for extrusion, the widened throat opening from the sweep, and rounded tips.
  8. FEA. Ran a Fusion 360 study using ASA material properties and a prescribed displacement equal to the installation deflection, to check the hand calculation and confirm where the part is actually loaded.
  9. Prototyping. Printed six prototypes. The first three were PLA, chosen purely for filament availability. I then switched to ABS, which sits in the same polymer family as ASA with comparable stiffness and strength, making it a reasonable mechanical stand-in — ASA's advantage over ABS is UV stability rather than mechanical performance.
  10. Testing. Installed and removed each prototype on the actual pipe and jacketing through 10 cycles, checking after each that the clip had not taken a permanent set severe enough to stop it clamping.
  11. Manufacturing and handoff. The constant cross section was chosen specifically to enable extrusion, but sourcing low-volume ASA extrusion at an acceptable price proved to be the real constraint on the project. My internship ended before extruded parts were ordered, so the design was handed off at the validated prototype stage.

Analysis and calculations

  • Force and moment balance establishing symmetric tip loads and a crown reaction of twice the tip load, reducing the clip to a single spring arm
  • Cantilever bending model with the arm length taken as arc length, L = Rθ, rather than a straight lever arm
  • Sizing relation t = 2·Sy·L² / (3·E·δ·FoS), written in terms of pipe radius and relaxed throat position so thickness and opening can be traded directly against one another
  • Arm stiffness k = 3EI/L³ used to convert the required deflection into an installation force, so the clip could be checked against what a hand can comfortably apply as well as against yield
  • Factor of safety computed across the full sweep rather than at a single point, producing a design window instead of one answer

Material selection

  • Plastic over metal, on the basis that a lower elastic modulus stores the required installation deflection at lower stress, with corrosion resistance as a second benefit
  • ASA specified for production for its UV stability in long-term outdoor exposure
  • ABS used for later prototypes as a mechanical stand-in for ASA, since the two are closely related and differ mainly in weathering performance
  • Material properties taken from published plastics property data rather than a supplier datasheet for a specific grade, which is the weakest input in the calculation chain and would be worth confirming before production

Manufacturing

  • Constant rectangular cross section, selected so the part could be extruded and cut to width rather than injection molded, avoiding tooling cost
  • Prototypes produced by FDM 3D printing
  • Sourcing low-volume ASA extrusion at an acceptable price was the limiting practical constraint on the project

Software and tools

  • Fusion 360 for CAD and finite element analysis
  • MATLAB for the parametric design sweep and plots
  • FDM 3D printing for prototypes

Test procedure

  1. Print the prototype and confirm the as-built geometry against the CAD model
  2. Spread the clip by hand and pass it over the 2.5 in nominal PVC pipe with the jacketing in place
  3. Confirm the clip snaps closed and holds the jacketing against the pipe
  4. Remove the clip by hand using the rounded tips
  5. Repeat for 10 install and removal cycles
  6. Inspect between cycles for cracking or permanent set; the pass criterion is that the clip still clamps the jacketing after the tenth cycle

Results

  • The sweep produced a defined design window rather than a single answer: at a 0.19 in wall thickness, 50 of the 200 throat openings evaluated (1.57 to 1.88 in) hold a factor of safety of at least 2.0 against yield during installation
  • At the selected design point, each arm deflects 0.47 in to clear the pipe, at a calculated installation force of 8.5 lbf total — low enough to install by hand, which was a requirement the stress calculation alone would not have caught
  • FEA returned a peak von Mises stress of 4,254 psi against a hand-calculated allowable of 4,250 psi, agreement within 0.09% and an implied factor of safety of 1.998 against the required 2.0. Two independent methods converging that closely gave real confidence in the sizing relation
  • FEA also confirmed bending as the governing failure mode and located the peak stress where the hand model predicted it
  • All six prototypes survived 10 install and removal cycles on the actual pipe and jacketing without taking a permanent set that compromised clamping [confirm whether the 10-cycle test was run on the PLA prototypes, the ABS prototypes, or both]
  • As-built prototype geometry was confirmed against the CAD model
  • Because the sizing script takes pipe diameter as an input, the same method regenerates geometry for larger pipe sizes; the built design targeted 2.5 in nominal, with larger diameters as the intended extension

Limitations

  • The first three prototypes were PLA, which at a prescribed deflection sees meaningfully higher stress than ASA would at the same geometry. Surviving in PLA is a conservative result, but it is not direct evidence about ASA
  • Printed parts are anisotropic and layer adhesion is orientation dependent, so the prototypes validate geometry, fit, and function rather than the mechanical performance of a finished extruded part
  • Testing covered 10 install cycles, not long-term outdoor exposure, UV degradation, or creep under sustained clamping load
  • Extruded ASA parts were never produced, so the production design is analytically and functionally validated but not manufacturing validated

What I took from it

The engineering that decided this project was not the stress analysis. Designing a geometry that was cheap to make in principle was straightforward; actually sourcing low-volume extrusion of the right material at an acceptable price was the part that stalled, and I would go looking for that constraint much earlier next time. The other thing I would keep is the sweep: solving for a window of valid geometry instead of one point meant I could move the throat opening later without redoing the analysis.
